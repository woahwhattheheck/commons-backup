# ClearFiber Shell — form-ready draft

**Challenge:** Starbucks Global Cold Cup Innovation Challenge  
**Working TRL:** 2–3 (material-system hypothesis plus published component precedents; no cup-scale prototype data is claimed)  
**Concept:** A transparent, plastic-free cup/lid material built from a dense cellulose-nanofibril (CNF) / chitosan physical network, protected by an ultra-thin natural shellac moisture barrier. The proposed process deliberately excludes chlorine bleach, PFAS, fossil polymers, and plant-based plastics that mimic conventional plastics.

> This is a technical proposal draft, not a claim that Starbucks' performance requirements have been met. Items marked `UNPROVEN` in `requirements-matrix.md` must remain unclaimed until measured on the final formed article.

## 1. Problem & Opportunity — <=500 words

Current clear cold-drink packaging gets its combination of optical clarity, stiffness, wet durability, impact performance and manufacturability largely from synthetic polymers. Fiber alternatives can reduce conventional plastic dependence but usually become opaque, swell in water, or require polymeric barrier layers. The opportunity is to treat transparency and wet strength as *microstructure* problems rather than reasons to reintroduce plastic.

ClearFiber Shell combines three natural-material mechanisms. First, well-dispersed cellulose nanofibrils can consolidate into dense films with fiber dimensions below visible-light scattering length scales. Second, chitosan physically cross-links CNF and has published precedent for preserving unusually high mechanical strength after full water soaking. Third, purified shellac is a natural lac resin with established food-contact listings and published use as a strong moisture barrier on cellulose packaging. The target architecture is therefore not “paper plus a plastic liner”; it is one coherent, plastic-free natural composite whose structural phase supplies strength and whose nanometric-to-micrometric surface phase slows water ingress.

The point of difference is the design-for-validation approach. We would screen optical haze first, because Starbucks’ <=5% ASTM D1003 target is substantially stricter than a generic claim of “transparent.” Only formulations clearing that gate would proceed to liquid hold, espresso-shot heat, drop, organoleptic, compostability and food-contact work. The same formulation and process would be frozen before certification testing, avoiding the common trap of proving each requirement on a different material.

The material is intentionally compatible with continuous aqueous CNF/chitosan preparation, roll-to-roll densification/coating, and matched-die or cup-former shaping rather than requiring a new petrochemical polymer platform. Cellulose, chitosan and shellac all have existing industrial supply chains, providing a credible route to hundreds-of-tons scale if the integrated article passes the hard performance gates.

The proposal is valuable even at TRL 2–3 because it defines a falsifiable route to a clear, compostable, plastic-free formed article: the critical uncertainties are explicit, measurable, and sequenced to kill weak formulations early rather than hiding them behind literature claims.

## 2. Solution Overview — <=500 words

The proposed article is a **ClearFiber Shell** laminate-free natural composite:

1. **Structural network:** highly purified, mechanically fibrillated cellulose nanofibrils blended with approximately 10–25 wt% chitosan. Acid-assisted dispersion is followed by controlled neutralization/drying so chitosan physically cross-links the CNF network. Published CNF/chitosan nanopaper achieved ~70–90% visible transmittance and, at 80/20 CNF/chitosan, ~100 MPa ultimate strength after water soaking. This is mechanism evidence, not cup performance.
2. **Optical densification:** filtration/dewatering plus calendering or matched-die hot pressing minimizes pores and refractive-index discontinuities. Feedstock and process control would prioritize low residual lignin/minerals and prohibit chlorine-bleach chemistry.
3. **Natural moisture barrier:** purified, dewaxed shellac applied as the thinnest continuous surface layer that survives forming. Recent shellac-on-cellulose studies report large reductions in water uptake/WVTR. FDA inventories list purified shellac in food-contact-related regulations, supporting a regulatory starting point but not a finished-cup authorization.
4. **Optional natural cross-link assist:** food-grade citric acid would be screened only if the physically cross-linked network cannot meet wet/heat targets. It would remain optional because every additive can change haze, odor, compostability and food-contact requirements.

The development program uses the Starbucks requirements as sequential acceptance gates. Stage A measures ASTM D1003 haze/transmittance on flat coupons and eliminates anything above 5% haze. Stage B measures 24-hour ice-water hold, dimensional change and wet compression. Stage C forms cup/lid prototypes and tests lid application, espresso-shot thermal deformation and 1 m filled-drop survival. Stage D runs migration/organoleptic screening and formal food-contact regulatory review. Stage E freezes the exact formulation for ASTM/BPI compostability testing and comparative LCA. Stage F transfers the winning process to continuous dewatering/densification/coating/forming equipment and measures throughput, scrap, drying energy and coating recovery.

No synthetic plastic, bioplastic mimic, PFAS, chlorine bleach, BPA, ortho-phthalate, oxodegradable additive, PVC/PVDC or PS/EPS is intentionally included. Supplier declarations plus analytical testing would be required before any “no measurable trace” claim.

## 3. Solution Feasibility / Supporting Rationale — <=500 words

The concept rests on complementary published precedents rather than a claim that one paper already solves the Starbucks specification.

**Wet structural strength.** Toivonen et al. (Biomacromolecules 2015, DOI 10.1021/acs.biomac.5b00145) physically cross-linked CNF with chitosan in water. Their 80/20 material retained ~100 MPa ultimate tensile strength after soaking and showed ~70–90% visible transmittance. Wu et al. (J. Mech. Behav. Biomed. Mater. 2014, DOI 10.1016/j.jmbbm.2014.01.014) likewise reported large wet-strength gains in transparent NFC/chitosan composites.

**Moisture barrier.** Poddar et al. (Int. J. Biol. Macromol. 2025, DOI 10.1016/j.ijbiomac.2025.143172) reported shellac-coated paper with WVTR down to ~56 g m^-2 day^-1 and Cobb water uptake ~1 g m^-2 at the strongest multilayer condition. A related 2025 study (DOI 10.1016/j.ijbiomac.2025.139855) reported >90% reduction in water absorption with optimized shellac coatings. These are planar-paper precedents; curved-cup durability must be measured.

**Food-contact starting point.** FDA’s Food Contact Substance inventory lists purified shellac (CAS 9000-59-3) with citations including 21 CFR 175.105, 175.300, 175.380 and 175.390. This does not automatically authorize the final composite or every intended use; an expert regulatory assessment and migration testing are required.

**Compostability path.** BPI’s current program uses ASTM D6400 as a base standard and offers ASTM D8410 assessment for eligible cellulosic-fiber packaging without thermoplastic lamination. ClearFiber Shell is designed to stay in that fiber-based, no-thermoplastic-laminate design space. Actual certification can require months of disintegration/biodegradation testing and therefore belongs after formulation freeze.

**Critical gap.** None of the cited sources demonstrates Starbucks’ complete requirement set, especially <=5% haze at cup thickness, 24-hour liquid integrity, filled 1 m drop survival and heat deformation. Those are go/no-go experiments, not assumptions. The proposal’s feasibility comes from combining credible mechanisms with an aggressive falsification sequence.

## 5. Solution Risks — <=500 words

1. **Haze is the dominant technical risk.** Published “transparent” CNF/chitosan films do not establish <=5% ASTM D1003 haze at final wall thickness or after forming. Mitigation: measure haze first on every candidate; control fibril size, solids, pore collapse, surface roughness and coating thickness; kill >5% formulations before expensive work.
2. **Water barrier vs. compostability.** More shellac or cross-linking may improve 24-hour integrity while slowing disintegration. Mitigation: DOE across minimal shellac add-on and CNF/chitosan ratio; freeze the thinnest barrier that passes use tests; then run BPI-directed testing on that exact formulation.
3. **Forming may destroy optics/barrier.** Draw strain can crack coatings, create wrinkles or increase scattering. Mitigation: compare pre-coat/post-form coat and warm-form sequences; inspect wall/corner/rim separately.
4. **Impact brittleness.** Dense cellulose structures can be stiff but notch-sensitive. Mitigation: tune chitosan fraction and wall geometry, add radiused features, and use filled 1 m drop as a hard gate.
5. **Heat/odor.** Espresso contact can soften hydrated networks or reveal shellac/chitosan odor. Mitigation: thermal deformation plus blinded organoleptic screening at relevant temperatures.
6. **Regulatory scope.** Ingredient listings are not a finished-article authorization. Mitigation: regulatory expert review, supplier specifications and migration testing before claiming suitability.
7. **Prohibited-substance contamination.** Conventional pulp bleaching/additives can violate the challenge even if not intentionally formulated. Mitigation: chlorine-bleach-free supplier route plus targeted analytical certificates/tests for the listed prohibited substances.
8. **Scale economics/energy.** Nanofibrillation and water removal can be energy intensive. Mitigation: quantify kWh/kg, solids recovery, drying load and coating solvent recovery in pilot work; compare against incumbent cup LCA rather than assuming environmental superiority.
9. **Raw-material variability.** Natural shellac/chitosan can vary. Mitigation: specification windows, dual-source qualification and incoming QC.

## 6. Timeline, Capability & Cost — <=500 words

This is a stage-gated development estimate, not a supplier quote.

**0–6 weeks — coupon kill-screen.** Prepare a bounded CNF/chitosan/shellac DOE; measure ASTM D1003 haze, tensile/wet tensile, water uptake, contact angle and thermal response. Exit only with <=5% haze candidates and no obvious odor/color failure. Estimated external-lab/material budget: **$15k–$40k** depending on existing equipment access.

**6–12 weeks — functional coupons and formed articles.** Optimize barrier add-on; run 24-hour ice-water containment/dimensional stability, wet compression, lid-application proxy, espresso-temperature deformation, and 1 m filled-drop tests. Form first cup/lid geometries using matched-die/wet-form/cup-former trials. Estimated **$25k–$75k**.

**3–6 months — regulatory and pilot validation.** Freeze formulation. Conduct food-contact regulatory assessment and required migration/sensory tests; generate prohibited-substance evidence; conduct pilot forming/coating throughput and yield trials. Estimated **$50k–$150k**, highly dependent on regulatory route and pilot access.

**6–18+ months — certification and scale transfer.** Enter BPI-directed compostability testing on the exact frozen article; BPI notes that biodegradation/disintegration testing can take many months. Run comparative ISO-style LCA and scale trials with a converter/material partner; validate hundreds-of-tons/year supply chain, QC and manufacturing economics. Estimated **$150k–$500k+** before commercial tooling, subject to partner assets and test scheme.

Capabilities required: nanocellulose dispersion/fibrillation, chitosan formulation, coating/solvent handling, densification/forming, ASTM D1003 optics, mechanical/thermal testing, food-contact regulatory expertise, compostability laboratory access, LCA and packaging-converter scale-up. The proposal does not claim the Solver currently owns all of these capabilities; partnering is expected where gaps exist.
